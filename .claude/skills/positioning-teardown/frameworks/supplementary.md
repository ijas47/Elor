# Supplementary lenses: Dunford, Laja, Ellis

These are pulled in **only when** the issue clearly belongs here and Pierri/Kaminski doesn't cover it. Default spine is `pierri-kaminski.md`.

## April Dunford. When to use

Use when the company is past PMF (>$5M ARR), is fighting a category battle, or the teardown is about **competitive frame** rather than copy.

### Positioning canvas
Five inputs, in order:
1. **Competitive alternatives**. What would the buyer use if you didn't exist? (Not always another product. Sometimes a spreadsheet, an agency, doing nothing.)
2. **Unique attributes**. What your product has that the alternatives don't.
3. **Value (and proof)**. What those attributes enable for the buyer.
4. **Best-fit customer**. Characteristics of the segment that values what you uniquely deliver.
5. **Market category**. The frame of reference. Pick the category where you look most differentiated.

### When to flag in a teardown
- The site doesn't name an alternative anywhere. Buyer can't tell who you replace.
- The category they claim makes them look weak (e.g., calling themselves "a CRM" when they have 3 features and HubSpot has 3,000).
- The "value" section reads as features, not buyer-value.

### Dunford's law
"If you don't position your product, your customers will. And they'll do a worse job."

## Peep Laja. When to use

Use for **conversion copy specificity** issues at the line level. Laja's lens is the close-read on individual sentences, not structural positioning.

### The 5-second test (Laja)
A first-time visitor should answer in 5 seconds:
1. What is this?
2. Who is it for?
3. What does it do?
4. Why should I care?
5. What do I do next?

Run this on the hero in the teardown. Score each 0–2.

### Specificity ladders

| Weak | Stronger | Strongest |
|---|---|---|
| "Save time" | "Save 10 hours a week" | "Replaces 4 hours/day of SDR data entry" |
| "Trusted by leaders" | "Used by 200+ Series B startups" | "How Ramp's RevOps team replaced 3 tools" |
| "AI-powered" | "GPT-4 fine-tuned on B2B sales emails" | "Claude + Clay enrichment, runs in 90 sec" |

In the teardown, find the weakest line on the page and write the strongest version next to it.

### Laja's clarity rule
"Clarity beats cleverness. Always." If a clever line slows comprehension, kill the cleverness.

## Sean Ellis. When to use

Use for **early-stage (pre-PMF) positioning** where the question is "are you even solving a must-have problem for your best users?"

### The PMF question
"How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?"
- Very disappointed
- Somewhat disappointed
- Not disappointed
- N/A

40% "very disappointed" = product-market fit signal.

### How it informs a teardown
If the company is pre-PMF, the homepage shouldn't be optimized for paid acquisition yet. It should be optimized for the **"very disappointed" segment's exact language**. Recommend extracting that language from customer interviews and putting it in the hero.

### The high-expectation customer
Position to the customer who would feel the most pain without you. Don't broaden the message to the average user. The hero should make the high-expectation customer feel seen; everyone else is collateral.

## Decision tree: which lens to pull from

- Hero copy is vague or platitudes → Pierri/Kaminski (Specificity Test)
- Wrong hero archetype for stage → Pierri/Kaminski (Hero archetypes)
- Buyer-awareness mismatch → Pierri/Kaminski (Schwartz/Kaminski)
- No named alternative, no competitive frame → Dunford
- Category claim is wrong / they look weak in their own category → Dunford
- Line-level specificity, weak verbs, vague numbers → Laja
- Pre-PMF and trying to write for everyone → Ellis (high-expectation customer)
- Pre-PMF and the hero copy doesn't sound like real customer language → Ellis ("very disappointed" segment)
